## What the numbers say (for parents)

**1. The score bar is a narrow band.** Composite SAT mid-50% sits roughly
1500–1580 and ACT 33–36 at every one of these 11 schools. A score below ~1500
SAT / 33 ACT is a meaningful outlier here, not a typical admit.

**2. For STEM, the SAT _math_ section is the real gate.** MIT's SAT Math mid-50%
runs about 780–800. STEM applicants get a little more slack on the reading/writing
half than on math — a math score that isn't near-perfect stands out the wrong way.

**3. Testing has swung back to required.** MIT, Caltech, Harvard, Yale (as
"test-flexible"), Penn, Dartmouth, and Brown all require scores; Stanford requires
them starting the Class of 2030. Only Columbia, Cornell, and Princeton were still
test-optional in their most recent published cycle. The practical takeaway: a
strong, submittable score is increasingly non-negotiable, and the test-optional
ranges above are upward-biased because only strong testers chose to submit.

**4. Grades and rank are a floor, not a differentiator.** 90%+ of admits sit in
the top 10% (often top 5%) of their high school class. Above that floor, the
admit decision is driven by the applicant's "spike," not by squeezing out the last
tenth of a GPA point.

**5. The STEM differentiator is concrete, externally-validated output.** Across
official "what we look for" guidance, the consistent signal is olympiad placement
(USAMO, USAPhO, USACO Platinum, USNCO), science-fair recognition (ISEF, Regeneron
STS), or published / faculty-mentored research — not club officer titles. Caltech
says this most bluntly: "depth" over breadth, and what you _produced_ (working
code, derived results, original engineering) over what position you held.

**6. Computer Science is the most crowded lane.** CS is the #1 or #2 most-conferred
major at essentially all 11 schools, so the CS applicant pool is the most
saturated. A USACO Gold/Platinum record or a shipped software project is closer to
table stakes than a bonus for CS-track applicants.

**7. Apply to the right division.** Cornell, Penn, and Princeton admit into
specific colleges/schools (Engineering, Wharton, SEAS), so a STEM spike should be
aimed at the correct division. Brown is the opposite case — its Open Curriculum
rewards a spike that bridges STEM with another field.

## Caveats

- CDS reports publish only 25th/75th composite percentiles for most of these
  schools, so the 50th percentile is frequently `null` in the data — not estimated.
- These schools report class-rank bands (CDS C11) rather than a mean GPA, so GPA
  notes describe rank distribution, not a single average.
- Test-optional-cycle SAT/ACT ranges (Columbia, Cornell, Princeton, Dartmouth's
  Class of 2028, Stanford's Class of 2028, Brown) overstate the typical admit
  because weaker testers withheld scores.
- EC / spike patterns are factual synthesis from official admissions guidance, not
  per-applicant statistics — treat them as direction, not a checklist.
- WebFetch on direct PDFs was unavailable in this environment; figures rest on
  search-indexed excerpts of the official CDS/OIR pages plus the cited aggregators.
  Spot-check headline numbers against the linked official CDS before quoting to a
  family.
