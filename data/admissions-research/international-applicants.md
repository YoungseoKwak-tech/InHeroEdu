# International applicants to top US universities — analysis

_Generated 2026-06-24. Every figure below is sourced in `international-applicants.json`; omit any number you cannot trace to that file's source URLs._

This stream looks at how admissions differ for **international applicants** (non-US citizens/residents applying from outside the US system) at the Ivy League plus MIT and Stanford. For Korean families using `/parents`, the single most decision-relevant variable here is the **need-blind vs. need-aware** split, so it leads.

## 1. Need-blind vs. need-aware for internationals — the two-tier reality

All of these schools meet **100% of demonstrated need** for admitted international students. The difference is whether a family's ability to pay can influence the *admit decision*:

| Policy for internationals | Schools |
| --- | --- |
| **Need-blind** (ability to pay does NOT affect admission) | Harvard, Princeton, Yale, MIT, Dartmouth (since Class of 2026), **Brown (new — from Class of 2029)** |
| **Need-aware** (ability to pay CAN affect admission) | Stanford, Columbia, Penn, Cornell |

- Stanford is the notable outlier: among Harvard, Princeton, Yale, MIT, and Dartmouth, **Stanford alone is not international need-blind**. Admitted internationals can still get full aid, but need can factor into the decision.
- Brown only became international need-blind starting with the **Class of 2029**, after meeting a $120M fundraising goal — making it one of roughly eight US schools that are international need-blind.
- Practical takeaway for families: at the need-aware four (Stanford/Columbia/Penn/Cornell), applying with little or no financial need is a genuine marginal advantage. At the need-blind six, it is not held against the applicant either way.

## 2. International admit rate vs. overall

Most top schools do **not** publish a separate international admit rate. Where they do, it is dramatically lower than the headline rate:

- **MIT (Class of 2029):** admitted **136 of 6,926 international applicants — under 2%**, versus an overall rate of ~4.6%.
- **Harvard (Class of 2028):** overall 3.59%; no separate international rate published.
- **Princeton (Class of 2028):** overall 4.6% (4.4% for Class of 2029); no separate international rate published.

Treat the overall admit rate as a **ceiling, not the realistic odds**, for international applicants.

## 3. Share of the class that is international (undergraduate)

**Caveat:** university-wide international figures include large graduate programs and are much higher (e.g. Columbia ~39% university-wide). Use the **undergraduate** number for college admissions context.

## 4. English-proficiency expectations (TOEFL / IELTS / Duolingo)

Expectations vary and are loosening at the top:

- **MIT:** undergrads from non-English-speaking countries do **not** have to submit TOEFL — only *recommended* if the student has used English fewer than 5 years or doesn't speak it regularly.
- **Stanford:** English-proficiency exams are **optional**; no preferred test. Reported recommended bands: TOEFL 100+ iBT, IELTS 7.0+, Duolingo 125+.
- **Princeton:** accepts TOEFL, IELTS Academic, **Duolingo (DET)**, or PTE; exempt if English is native or the student spent 3+ years at an English-instruction secondary school. No published undergrad minimum.
- **Columbia:** hard minimum **TOEFL 105 iBT** (for exams on/before Jan 20, 2026).
- **Cornell:** hard minimum **TOEFL 100 iBT** (for exams before Jan 2026).

Note: TOEFL score scales change in **January 2026** — applicants should verify the current band/scale before testing.

## 5. What differentiates strong international applicants

- **Borderless distinction travels best.** International Olympiad medals (IMO, IOI, IPhO) and national-team selection are recognized across borders — MIT, Harvard, and Stanford actively recognize IMO medalists. National-team selection rates are extraordinarily low (IMO national-qualifier selection ~0.038% globally), which is precisely why the signal is strong.
- **Frame achievements against US standards.** Admissions officers understand the US competition landscape best, so internationals should explicitly contextualize honors relative to their country's selectivity (e.g., "selected to the 6-person national team out of N participants") rather than assuming the committee knows the local hierarchy.
- **Holistic, not single-trophy.** Even a top Olympiad medal rarely decides a case alone; it functions as one exceptional spike within an otherwise strong holistic file.

## Caveats

- Several schools publish only an *overall* admit rate; international-specific rates beyond MIT are not disclosed and were intentionally left `null` rather than estimated.
- Class-share percentages drift year to year and by whether a source counts admits vs. enrolled — figures here are tagged to a cycle where possible.
- Policy facts (need-blind status, TOEFL minimums) change; Brown's need-blind shift (Class of 2029) and the Jan 2026 TOEFL scale change are recent. Re-verify before advising a family.
